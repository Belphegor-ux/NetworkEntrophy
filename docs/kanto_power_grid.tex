\begin{abstract}
We study the Kanto region high-voltage power transmission network --- the
\SI{500}{\kilo\volt} and \SI{275}{\kilo\volt} backbone operated by TEPCO and
J-Power across Eastern Japan --- as a complex network and rank its edges by
structural criticality. Using the largest connected component
(\num{156} nodes, \num{190} edges) extracted from OpenStreetMap via the
ComplexNetTSP power-grids dataset, we apply nine edge-ranking metrics
covering local degree (LDC), neighbourhood overlap (Jaccard), $k$-shell
(LKS), Collective Influence (four variants), Link-Local Betweenness
Centrality (LLBCe) and Link-Local Betweenness Mapping Entropy (LLBMEe1).
Each metric is evaluated by progressive edge removal and the area under the
relative-giant-component (RGC) curve. In the static regime, LLBCe attains
the lowest AUC ($0.174$), outperforming the next-best metric (Jaccard,
$0.220$) by \SI{21}{\percent}; in the iterative regime, where ranks are
recomputed after each removal, CI variants improve substantially while
LLBCe retains its lead by a smaller margin. We further inventory the
network by named substation, identify the seven trunk hubs that anchor the
\SI{500}{\kilo\volt} grid, and discuss the line of bridges connecting the
TEPCO service area to peripheral regions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Power transmission grids are canonical examples of critical infrastructure
whose failure modes are dictated as much by network topology as by the
electromechanical properties of any single component. The 2003 North
American blackout, the 2011 T\=ohoku earthquake aftermath, and the 2018
Taiwan brownout each highlighted that a small number of structurally
exposed transmission corridors can disconnect tens of millions of customers
when removed in sequence~\cite{albert2004structural,buldyrev2010catastrophic}.

In Japan, the Kanto region --- centred on Tokyo and bounded approximately
by longitudes $138.3^{\circ}$E to $141.0^{\circ}$E and latitudes
$34.8^{\circ}$N to $37.1^{\circ}$N --- contains the highest electricity
demand density in the country, served entirely at \SI{50}{\hertz} by Tokyo
Electric Power Company (TEPCO) and Electric Power Development
(``J-Power'')~\cite{tepco2024network}. Frequency conversion to the western
\SI{60}{\hertz} grid is bottlenecked through three back-to-back DC
substations with limited capacity, so internal Kanto resilience effectively
determines black-start capability for the eastern half of the country.

This paper applies the network-dismantling framework to the Kanto
\SI{500}{\kilo\volt}/\SI{275}{\kilo\volt} backbone and asks: \emph{which
transmission edges, if removed, most rapidly fragment the bulk-power
network?} We compare nine edge-ranking metrics drawn from recent literature
on link criticality, evaluate them under both a static (one-shot ordering)
and an iterative (rank-then-remove-then-recompute) regime, and report the
implications for vulnerability assessment.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Edge ranking and network dismantling}
The dismantling problem~\cite{braunstein2016network} asks for the smallest
set of nodes (or edges) whose removal reduces the size of the largest
connected component below a target threshold. For edge variants, ranking
heuristics are typically evaluated by the relative-giant-component (RGC)
curve as a function of removed-edge fraction, and summarised by the area
under the curve (AUC):
\begin{equation}
\text{AUC}(M) = \int_0^1 \text{RGC}\bigl(f \mid \text{order}_M\bigr)\, df
\label{eq:auc}
\end{equation}
where $\text{order}_M$ is the removal order induced by metric $M$. Lower
AUC implies a metric that identifies critical edges earlier in the removal
sequence.

\subsection{Power-grid topology}
GridKit~\cite{wiegmans2016gridkit} extracted high-voltage transmission
networks from OpenStreetMap (OSM) for Europe and North America, but does
\emph{not} cover Japan. The
ComplexNetTSP/Power\_grids project~\cite{complexnettsp2023} produced an
analogous extraction for Japan directly from raw OSM \texttt{power=*} tags,
yielding two CSVs: one for vertices (substations and line-segment
junctions) and one for edges (transmission lines). We use this source.

\subsection{Edge criticality metrics}
We evaluate nine metrics from three families. \textbf{Topological:}
Link Degree Centrality (LDC, $k_u \cdot k_v$), Jaccard index of
neighbourhood overlap, and Link $k$-Shell (LKS, $\text{core}(u) \cdot
\text{core}(v)$). \textbf{Influence-based:} four variants of edge-level
Collective Influence (CI) at radius $\ell{=}3$, distinguishing the ball
``skin'' (nodes at exactly distance $\ell$) from the ball ``body'' (nodes
within distance $\ell$), aggregated to edges by either average or product
of endpoint scores~\cite{morone2015influence,kitsak2024edge}.
\textbf{Entropy-based:} Link-Local Betweenness Centrality (LLBCe) and
Link-Local Betweenness Mapping Entropy
(LLBMEe1)~\cite{li2024llbce}. The latter two are recent and require careful
implementation; see \Cref{sec:methods}.

\section{Data and Network Construction}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source data}
We start from \texttt{japan\_nodes.csv} and \texttt{japan\_edges.csv} of
the ComplexNetTSP Power\_grids release. Vertices carry \texttt{v\_id},
longitude/latitude (WGS-84, EPSG:4326), \texttt{voltage} (volts),
\texttt{frequency}, \texttt{name} (Japanese, with romaji where present),
\texttt{operator}, and \texttt{typ} (substation, generator, joint, etc.).
Edges carry endpoint pairs.

\subsection{Geographic filter and largest connected component}
We restrict to a Kanto bounding box
\(\text{lon} \in [138.3, 141.0],\ \text{lat} \in [34.8, 37.1]\),
and extract the largest connected component (LCC) of the resulting subgraph.
This yields:
\begin{itemize}
\item \num{156} nodes (\num{99} named substations or labelled junctions,
\num{57} unlabelled OSM line-segment vertices),
\item \num{190} edges,
\item \num{56} bridges and \num{54} articulation points,
\item diameter \num{23},
\item degree distribution
$[\,(1,29),(2,66),(3,39),(4,14),(5,3),(6,4),(7,1)\,]$
yielding mean degree $\bar k = \num{2.44}$.
\end{itemize}
All retained nodes operate at \SI{50}{\hertz}; despite the loose
longitudinal bound, no Chubu (\SI{60}{\hertz}) substation is included. The
Eastern--Western frequency boundary at the Fuji and Sakuma converter
stations falls just outside the western edge of the box.

\subsection{Verification}
A spot-check of \num{10}/\num{10} randomly chosen named substations
confirmed real TEPCO/J-Power facilities at the listed coordinates,
including Shin-Tokorozawa, Shin-Tama, Shin-Keiyo, Shin-Hadano,
Shin-Okabe, B\=os\=o, Shin-Haruna, Shin-Sawara, Katsunan, and
Shin-Fuji. Top-degree hubs were verified against TEPCO's published
\SI{500}{\kilo\volt} backbone illustration~\cite{tepco2024network}.

\subsection{Caveats}
Of \num{156} LCC nodes, \num{57} are unlabelled OSM line-segment
junctions: places where the cartographer split a transmission corridor
into multiple OSM ways for tagging or vertex-density reasons, but which
do not correspond to a substation. These inflate the apparent
diameter and contribute to the \num{56} bridges. We retain them because
removing them changes edge counts and biases comparisons; readers
interested in the substation-only topology can collapse them by merging
degree-2 unnamed vertices.

\subsection{Regional decomposition}
A coarse classification by lon/lat box (\Cref{tab:regions}) confirms the
network's centroid in the Tokyo Metropolitan area, with \num{35}
\SI{}{\percent} of edges in the Tokyo Metro West/East
combined and \num{18}\,\% as inter-region transmission corridors that
form the most exposed bridges.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Notation and conventions}
Let $G = (V, E)$ with $|V| = N$ and $|E| = m$. Let $k_v = \deg_G(v)$ and
$N(v) = \{u : (u,v) \in E\}$. All metrics produce a Pandas DataFrame with
columns $\{i, j, M_1, \dots, M_q\}$, where $(i,j)$ is the edge and
$M_1, \dots, M_q$ are scalar scores.

\subsection{Topological metrics}
\begin{align}
\text{LDC}(u,v)     &= k_u \cdot k_v, \\
\text{Jaccard}(u,v) &= \frac{|N(u) \cap N(v)|}{|N(u) \cup N(v)|}, \\
\text{LKS}(u,v)     &= \text{core}(u) \cdot \text{core}(v),
\end{align}
where $\text{core}(\cdot)$ is the $k$-core index from peeling.

\subsection{Collective Influence (four variants)}
For radius $\ell = 3$, define the ball boundary
$\partial \text{Ball}(u, \ell) = \{w : d_G(u,w) = \ell\}$ and the ball
volume $\text{Ball}(u, \ell) = \{w : 0 < d_G(u,w) \le \ell\}$. The
node-level CI is then
\begin{equation}
\text{CI}_S(u) = (k_u - 1) \sum_{w \in \partial\text{Ball}(u, \ell)} (k_w - 1),
\quad
\text{CI}_B(u) = (k_u - 1) \sum_{w \in \text{Ball}(u, \ell)} (k_w - 1).
\end{equation}
These are aggregated to edges by either arithmetic mean or product of
endpoint scores, yielding four edge-level columns:
$\text{CI\_e\_av\_skin}, \text{CI\_e\_mul\_skin},
\text{CI\_e\_av\_body}, \text{CI\_e\_mul\_body}$.

\subsection{LLBCe and LLBMEe1}
For an edge $e = (u,v)$, define the first-order central domain
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(e) \;=\; \{u, v\} \,\cup\, N(u) \,\cup\, N(v).
\end{equation}
The Link-Local Betweenness Centrality is then
\begin{equation}
\text{LLBCe}(e) \;=\; \frac{1}{|\Gamma(e)|\bigl(|\Gamma(e)| - 1\bigr)}
\sum_{\substack{s,t \in \Gamma(e) \\ s \ne t}}
\frac{\sigma_{st}(e)}{\sigma_{st}},
\label{eq:llbce}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{st}$ is the number of shortest $s$--$t$ paths in $G$ and
$\sigma_{st}(e)$ the number passing through $e$. Crucially, the source
and target sets are restricted to $\Gamma(e)$, but path computation
remains in the full graph $G$ --- subgraph extraction is forbidden, as
that would change shortest-path lengths and yield qualitatively
different rankings. We implement \Cref{eq:llbce} via NetworkX's
\texttt{edge\_betweenness\_centrality\_subset} with both \texttt{sources}
and \texttt{targets} set to $\Gamma(e)$, and divide the unnormalised
output by $|\Gamma(e)|(|\Gamma(e)|{-}1)$.

The Link-Local Betweenness Mapping Entropy LLBMEe1 evaluates the
distributional concentration of LLBCe values over edges incident to
either endpoint of $e_1$:
\begin{equation}
\text{LLBMEe1}(e_1) = -\sum_{e_k \in \Gamma_E(e_1)}
p_k \log p_k,
\quad
p_k = \frac{\text{LLBCe}(e_k)}{\sum_{e_j \in \Gamma_E(e_1)} \text{LLBCe}(e_j)},
\label{eq:llbme}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_E(e_1)$ is the set of edges incident to $u$ or $v$. Edges
with concentrated mass (a single dominant neighbour) yield low LLBMEe1;
edges in homogeneous bottlenecks yield high LLBMEe1.

\subsection{Static and iterative dismantling}
Given a metric $M$, the \emph{static} curve sorts edges once, removes
them in that order, and records RGC after each removal. The
\emph{iterative} curve recomputes $M$ on the residual graph after every
removal, picks the current top edge, and proceeds. AUC is computed via
$\texttt{numpy.trapezoid}$.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Static AUC ranking on Kanto}
\Cref{tab:tokyo-static} gives the static AUC for each metric on the Kanto
LCC. LLBCe attains the lowest AUC ($0.174$), markedly better than the
next group of metrics (Jaccard, LLBMEe1, LKS, LDC, all in the range
$0.220$--$0.238$), and notably better than the four CI variants
($0.251$--$0.275$). The latter is a known
phenomenon~\cite{kitsak2024edge}: CI's targeting of bulk influence on
nodes does not necessarily map to bridge-style transmission corridors,
which are precisely the structures that fragment a sparse \SI{500}{\kilo\volt}
backbone.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why LLBCe wins}
The Kanto grid is a sparse ($\bar k = 2.44$), tree-like backbone with
\num{56} bridges and diameter \num{23}. In such networks, global
betweenness on the full graph is dominated by a handful of edges that
sit on long structural paths --- but it is too coarse to discriminate
between them. LLBCe's restriction of source/target sets to the
edge's first-order neighbourhood $\Gamma(e)$ acts as a local
``importance weighting'' that effectively asks: \emph{among the
\num{2}--\num{12} substations within one hop of this edge, what
fraction of their pairwise shortest paths actually use it?} This both
rescales scores to the local edge-density (so a bridge in a dense
metropolitan cluster scores comparably to one in a sparse rural area)
and remains computable on every edge in the order of seconds even with
\num{156} nodes.

\subsection{Why CI underperforms statically}
CI was designed for node-level immunisation in scale-free networks with
heavy degree tails. The Kanto grid has degree distribution capped at
\num{7} (no hub-and-spoke superhub), so CI's preferential weighting of
high-degree nodes inside the ball does not translate cleanly to edge
ranks. Iteratively recomputing CI rescues much of this performance
($-25\,\%$ relative AUC), confirming that the static failure is
information-theoretic (the CI score becomes stale immediately after the
first removal) rather than methodological.

\subsection{Practical implications}
The seven trunk hubs identified
(\Cref{fig:kanto-map}) are precisely the substations that TEPCO
publicly designates as
\SI{500}{\kilo\volt} interconnection points~\cite{tepco2024network}.
The top-ten LLBCe edges (\Cref{tab:top-edges}) all carry combinations
of \SI{275}{\kilo\volt} and \SI{500}{\kilo\volt} feeders. Operators
performing $N{-}1$ contingency analysis on this network should
prioritise these corridors; planners considering trunk reinforcements
should view the Shin-Koga $\leftrightarrow$ Kita-Tokyo edge as
the most structurally exposed single-line failure point.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:validity}

\paragraph{OSM completeness.}
The ComplexNetTSP extraction is bounded by what OSM contributors have
mapped. We verified \num{10}/\num{10} named substations against TEPCO's
illustrated facility plan, but the \num{57} unnamed line-segment vertices
suggest that \emph{some} segments may be over-fragmented relative to
their physical topology. Authoritative data from TEPCO is not publicly
released as a graph; the closest published proxy is Toyoda et
al.~\cite{toyoda2008japan}.

\paragraph{No electrical model.}
We treat the network as a topological graph with unit weights. A
production vulnerability assessment would weight edges by line
admittance, capacity, and seasonal load profile. The metrics here
identify topologically critical edges, not necessarily electrically
critical ones; the two coincide approximately but not exactly.

\paragraph{Tied-path non-determinism.}
NetworkX's \texttt{edge\_betweenness\_centrality\_subset} resolves
shortest-path ties in dict-iteration order. For LLBCe values where many
paths tie (common in trunk corridors with parallel \SI{500}{\kilo\volt}
and \SI{275}{\kilo\volt} segments), values may differ at the third or
fourth significant figure across runs. Rankings are stable, but exact
AUC reproduction requires explicit sorting of node iteration.

\paragraph{Frequency boundary.}
The bounding box leaks slightly west of the \SI{50}{}/\SI{60}{\hertz}
divide; in practice no Chubu (\SI{60}{\hertz}) node is included, but
this is by data luck, not by an explicit frequency filter. A second
production filter on \texttt{frequency = 50} would harden the
boundary.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The Kanto \SI{500}{\kilo\volt}/\SI{275}{\kilo\volt} backbone, despite
serving the world's most populous metropolitan area, presents as a
remarkably sparse network whose vulnerability is dominated by a small
number of bridge corridors. Across nine edge-criticality metrics
evaluated under both static and iterative dismantling regimes,
Link-Local Betweenness Centrality (LLBCe) emerges as the most effective
ranker, with static AUC $= 0.174$ and stable iterative performance.
The top critical edges identified --- chiefly along the Shin-Koga,
Shin-Tokorozawa, and Shin-Keiyo trunks --- align with TEPCO's published
\SI{500}{\kilo\volt} interconnection map, validating the pure-topology
approach. Future work should fuse the topology with line-admittance
and capacity data to deliver an electrically-aware criticality score.
